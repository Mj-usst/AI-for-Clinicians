# Instructor guide · Run a 55-minute evidence-checking lesson

This lesson is for clinicians with no coding experience. The aims are to verify literature, identify comparison conditions and write conclusions with clear boundaries. The teaching pilot and evaluation methods below are **proposed plans**. The course reports no completed pilot results or validated assessment scale.

## Prepare before class

- Read the [lesson](README.en.md), [source cards](sources.en.md), [practice](practice.en.md) and [answers](answer-key.en.md). Check that the published-version links and indicated sections for P1/P2/P3 open. Prioritize setting, sample units, comparisons and outcomes.
- Give each learner a [task card](../../templates/task-card.en.md), [evidence table](../../templates/evidence-table.en.csv), [search log](../../templates/search-log.en.csv) and [evidence checklist](../../templates/evidence-checklist.en.md). Print them or download them for a local spreadsheet.
- Keep the practice page's **course-written, deliberately inaccurate text** notice visible before showing the summary. Retain E1–E6 throughout. Open the answers only after learners complete the exercise.
- Use only public papers and course material. Learners need no patient data, coding environment, paid model or AI account. Optional AI tools organize public material; registration during class and full-text uploads are not required.

## Guide six tasks over 55 minutes

1. **0–5 minutes · Set the task and distinguish the units.** Show the teaching question. Ask learners to explain clinicians versus cases, model alone versus clinicians using AI, and task scores versus patient outcomes. Explain that the papers are fixed teaching selections, not a systematic review.
2. **5–13 minutes · Write a task card and record a search.** Specify population, task, intervention, comparator and outcome. Demonstrate the example query. If online, record the actual date and result count. Without access, mark **Not run** and leave the count blank rather than substituting an example number.
3. **13–25 minutes · Work through P1.** Read the original sections named on the card and complete one evidence row. Have learners say each number's unit. Keep the group scores, adjusted difference and confidence interval separate. Demonstrate an honest verification status.
4. **25–35 minutes · Compare P2 and P3.** Learners complete a P2 row, identifying the assistance physicians actually received and its comparator. Then identify only P3's setting and comparison, discussing whether it is direct evidence or background for the task card. Keep the sample independent conclusion hidden.
5. **35–50 minutes · Correct and submit independently.** Allow about 8 minutes for E1–E6 and 7 minutes to complete P3's independent row from material already read and write a **150–200 English-word conclusion**. Save first drafts before exchanging work for checking. Record which items required prompts.
6. **50–55 minutes · Give feedback and revise.** Open the answers for self- or peer scoring. Check E2/E3/E6 before the total. Ask each learner to record one judgment before and after correction, retaining both the first draft and revision.

The English assignment counts **English words**, not Chinese characters. The Chinese version has its own Chinese-character requirement. Use the practice page's whitespace-based word count; the sample answer contains **171 English words** under that rule.

This timetable assumes materials are ready in advance and learners read only the specified sections during class. For a **45-minute version**, the instructor can show a completed task card and an authentic recorded search while retaining the corrections and independent assignment. For a **60-minute version**, add five minutes for finding original locations and completing source notes.

Keep unread fields unverified even when time is short. If most learners cannot finish, record actual time and let them complete the work afterwards; do not report that all objectives were achieved.

## Use the offline or no-account route

Save the course Markdown and blank workbooks before class, and prepare public entry points or permitted short extracts. Printed source cards, exercises and paper tables can support the session directly. Course cards are course paraphrases, not a learner's original-source check. Relevant rows should say **Course card only; original text not yet checked**. Leave an unrun search count blank, explain why it was not run, and check it once access returns.

An AI response does not need to be generated live. The inaccurate text was written for this course and can already train evidence checking; do not present it as a real output from a named model. Offline learners can still correct errors, compare designs and write bounded conclusions while clearly recording their verification level.

## Give prompts without replacing independent work

First ask:

- “What does this number count?”
- “Who is in each group, and what can they use?”
- “Were real patient outcomes observed?”

Then guide the learner to the relevant original location. Do not supply an answer and record the resulting work as independently completed.

Use the same [20-point rubric](answer-key.en.md): the suggested pass requires at least **16/20**, full **2-point corrections for each of E2/E3/E6**, and no repetition of those errors in the independent conclusion. Work based only on course cards can use the same rubric when labelled honestly. Record original-source and card-only completion separately.

Mark prompted work **Completed after prompting**. Retain the first independent score separately from the revised score. If two instructors are available, they may score the same submission independently, discuss disagreements and agree how to apply the rubric. That step is not scale validation.

## Plan a small voluntary pilot · 5–10 clinicians

Invite 5–10 clinicians with no coding experience who wish to try the lesson. Explain the content, expected time and intended use of feedback beforehand. Participation must not affect work appraisal. Use assigned learner codes; collect no patient information. Names are unnecessary for saving teaching feedback.

This small sample can reveal problems in materials and delivery. It cannot establish that the course is effective or represents all clinicians' needs.

For each learner, consider recording the fields below. Leave a field blank with an explanation when an event did not occur or was not observed:

```text
Learner code:
Date and material version:
Route: AI-assisted / no AI; original text online / offline cards
Actual start and completion times; unfinished tasks:
First independent score: __/20; full points on each of E2/E3/E6:
Met the suggested pass rule on first attempt:
Prompts required (number, task, content); work completed after prompting:
Revised score and whether the critical errors were corrected:
Original locations not found; links that would not open; unclear terms:
Voluntary learner feedback:
```

Before the pilot, learners could explain aloud how they would check an AI literature summary. Afterwards, use the assignment to see whether they can perform those actions. These are different tasks; differences between them are not evidence from equally difficult pre- and post-tests. A later retention assessment would need a separate task of similar difficulty, with scoring and delivery specified in advance.

## Collect feedback and improve the materials

Ask three optional questions after the session:

1. “Which step was hardest, and exactly where did you get stuck?”
2. “Which judgment changed after you checked the original source?”
3. “Next time you see a literature summary, could you record the sources and verification status yourself?”

Learners may skip questions. Summarize actual completions/participants, the range of actual time taken, first and revised scores, specific E2/E3/E6 errors, and tasks where help was commonly needed. Record whether AI was used and whether learners used online originals or offline cards. Do not combine these routes into a comparison of tool effects. Leave unobserved data unfilled; do not invent satisfaction rates, time-saving percentages or teaching testimonials.

Prioritize broken source links, locations learners cannot find and unclear exercise instructions. Record the revision date, problem and change, then ask a few learners to check the revised material. Expand later lessons only when pilot records support doing so. A small exploratory pilot must not be presented as established training effectiveness.
